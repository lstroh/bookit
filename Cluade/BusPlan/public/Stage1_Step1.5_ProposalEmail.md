# Stage 1 — Step 1.5: The Proposal Email

**Status:** Active Reference
**Applies To:** All new client proposals — Professional Plan

## What This Step Is

The proposal email is where the sale is made or lost on paper. Done well, it reads like a message from someone who genuinely understood what the prospect told them on the call — not a template, not a PDF attached to a one-line email, not a generic brochure.

The prospect should read it and think: *"This is exactly what I described. They were listening."*

You have one job with this email: make saying yes feel obvious and easy.

---

## Timing

Send within 24 hours of the discovery call. The call is when their interest is highest and their pain is freshest. Every hour that passes reduces both.

If you set the Bonsai task correctly in Step 1.4, you have a reminder for tomorrow morning. Do not let it slip.

---

## Format

This is a personal email — not a PDF attachment, not a slide deck, not a formal proposal document. A clean, well-structured email that reads like it was written specifically for this person, because it was.

**Why not a PDF?**
- PDFs create friction — they have to open it, read it in a different context, and come back to reply
- A well-written email is read in the moment, on any device, and replied to immediately
- PDFs feel like they came from a template. Emails feel like they came from a person.

**Length:** Aim for something they can read in 3–4 minutes. Long enough to be thorough, short enough to hold attention.

---

## The Structure

### 1. Subject Line

Keep it personal and specific — not generic.

**Good:**
- *"Your booking website — proposal for Shine & Style"*
- *"Proposal for Active Body Physio — as discussed"*
- *"Following up — booking site proposal for [Business Name]"*

**Avoid:**
- *"Proposal"* (too generic, looks like a template)
- *"Following up on our call"* (no context)
- *"Wimbledon Smart Business — Website Proposal"* (about you, not them)

---

### 2. Opening Paragraph — Reflect Back What You Heard

This is the most important paragraph in the email. Open by summarising what you heard on the call — in their language, not yours. This signals immediately that you were paying attention and that this proposal is specific to them.

Use the pain point you captured in Bonsai. Use their words where possible.

**Template:**
> *"Really enjoyed speaking with you [yesterday / on [day]]. From what you described, [restate their situation in one or two sentences — current booking setup, the main frustration, what they want to fix]. Here's what I'd build for you."*

**Example for a salon owner:**
> *"Really enjoyed speaking with you yesterday. From what you described, you're currently managing all bookings by phone and WhatsApp, which is eating into your time behind the chair — and you're losing bookings outside business hours because there's no way for clients to book themselves in. Here's what I'd build for you."*

**Example for a physiotherapist:**
> *"Great speaking with you on Thursday. You're currently on Calendly across four therapists, but it doesn't handle recurring appointments well and clients keep calling to book their weekly slots manually anyway. Here's what I'd propose."*

Do not start with "I hope this email finds you well." Do not start with "Thank you for taking the time to speak with me." Get straight to the point.

---

### 3. What I Will Build — Specific to Them

Not a feature list copied from your website. A description of what you will actually build for this specific business, using the details they gave you on the call.

**Template:**
> *"For [Business Name], I'd build:"*

Then list the specifics. Keep it tight — 6–8 bullet points maximum.

**Example:**
> *"For Shine & Style, I'd build:*
> *- A professional 6-page website in your brand colours and existing logo*
> *- An online booking system showing availability for all 4 stylists*
> *- Services listed with duration and pricing (your 8 services as discussed)*
> *- Automated email reminders sent 24 hours before each appointment*
> *- A £15 deposit collected automatically at the time of booking via Stripe*
> *- A separate dashboard your team logs into — not WordPress admin*
> *- Google Calendar sync so each stylist sees their bookings in their own calendar"*

This level of specificity does two things: it proves you listened, and it reduces the prospect's mental effort in evaluating the proposal because they can picture exactly what they are getting.

---

### 4. What Is Included

One short paragraph or a brief list covering what is included in the ongoing monthly fee. Keep it simple — they do not need the full feature list here.

**Example:**
> *"The monthly fee covers hosting on a fast UK server, all software and security updates, daily backups, and email support. You won't need to touch WordPress or deal with technical issues — that is all handled."*

---

### 5. Pricing — Clear and Confident

State the pricing clearly. Do not bury it, do not over-explain it, do not apologise for it. Present both options (monthly and annual) and let them choose.

**Template:**

> *"Pricing for [Business Name]:*
>
> *Monthly billing:*
> *£995 setup fee (one-off) + £99/month*
> *Year 1 total: £2,183*
>
> *Annual billing (recommended):*
> *£495 setup fee (one-off) + £1,188/year*
> *Year 1 total: £1,683 — saving £500*
>
> *Both options are month-to-month after the first year with 30 days notice to cancel. No long-term lock-in."*

**If applying introductory pricing (first two clients):**

> *"As one of my first clients, I'm offering a reduced setup fee of £495 (instead of £995) in exchange for a short testimonial and case study once you've been live for a few weeks. The monthly fee stays at £99. Year 1 total: £1,683."*

---

### 6. Payment Milestones

A short paragraph or simple table. Keeps things transparent and professional.

**Template:**
> *"Payment is split across three milestones:*
> *- 10% (£99.50) to get started — due when you sign the contract*
> *- 40% (£398) at design review — when I send you the first version to look at*
> *- 50% (£497.50) before go-live — once you've approved the final site*
> *Monthly billing starts from launch date."*

---

### 7. Timeline

One sentence. Clear and specific.

> *"From the point I receive your brief and assets, the site will be live within 6 weeks."*

If they mentioned a specific deadline on the call, reference it:

> *"You mentioned wanting to be live before the summer season — that is entirely achievable if we get started in the next couple of weeks."*

---

### 8. What Happens Next — Single Clear CTA

One action. Not two. Not three.

> *"If you'd like to go ahead, just reply to this email and I'll send the contract and first invoice over the same day. If you have any questions, reply here or call me on [number] and we can go through it together."*

Do not offer too many options. Do not say "let me know your thoughts." Do not say "feel free to reach out if you have questions" — it sounds like you do not expect a reply.

## Variations for Common Situations

### Prospect with no logo or brand assets

Add a short note after the "What I'd build" section:

> *"One thing worth flagging: you mentioned you don't have a logo yet. I can recommend a designer, or we can use placeholder branding to build the site and swap it in later — whichever works best for you. Happy to talk through options."*

### Prospect who asked about a feature not yet built

Address it directly rather than hoping they forgot:

> *"You asked about [feature] on the call — that's not in the current version but is on the roadmap for later this year. In the meantime, [practical workaround]. I'd flag it when it's ready if you'd like."*

### Prospect who is clearly price-sensitive

Lead slightly harder on the annual option and the value framing:

> *"I'd recommend the annual plan — the setup fee drops by £500 and Year 1 comes in at £1,683 total. To put that in context: if you're currently on Fresha and bringing in £150/week in new clients, you're paying Fresha around £1,560 a year in commission alone. With us, you keep all of it."*

### Prospect who mentioned an existing website they want to keep

> *"You mentioned you're happy with parts of your current site — happy to work with what you have rather than rebuilding from scratch. Once I see the site I can advise on the cleanest way to integrate the booking system."*

## After Sending

Set the three Bonsai follow-up tasks the moment you send the proposal:

| Task | Due |
|---|---|
| Day 3 nudge email | 3 days after proposal sent |
| Day 7 phone call | 7 days after proposal sent |
| Day 14 final email + mark Lost if no response | 14 days after proposal sent |

Update Bonsai status to **Proposal Sent**.

**Day 3 nudge:**
> *"Hi [Name], just checking this landed okay — happy to answer any questions if anything needs clarifying. [Booking link if they want to call]"*

**Day 7 voicemail (if no answer):**
> *"Hi [Name], Liron here from Wimbledon Smart — just following up on the proposal I sent over. Happy to answer any questions. You can reach me on [number] or just reply to the email."*

**Day 14 final email:**
> *"Hi [Name], I'll assume the timing isn't right for now — no problem at all. If things change down the line, feel free to get in touch. The proposal stands."*

Then mark as **Lost — No Response** in Bonsai. Do not chase further.

## What Makes a Proposal Win

Based on everything in this process, a proposal converts when it does these four things:

**1. It opens with their words, not yours.**
The first paragraph should make them think "they were actually listening." If you could swap the prospect's name out and send the same email to someone else, it is not personal enough.

**2. It describes what you will build specifically.**
"A professional website with booking" is generic. "A 6-page site for your four stylists with £15 deposit collection and Google Calendar sync" is a proposal.

**3. It makes the pricing feel fair in context.**
Always present both options. Always include the "save £500" framing for annual. If price sensitivity was flagged in your Bonsai notes, add the Fresha or Bookly comparison.

**4. It ends with one clear action.**
Reply to go ahead. That is it. Everything else — the contract, the questionnaire, the invoice — comes after they say yes. Do not put all of it in the proposal email.

- [ ] Proposal sent within 24 hours of the discovery call
- [ ] Subject line is specific to this prospect — not generic
- [ ] Opening paragraph restates their situation in their words
- [ ] What I will build section is specific to this business — not a generic feature list
- [ ] Both pricing options presented clearly (monthly and annual)
- [ ] Introductory pricing applied if applicable (first two clients)
- [ ] Payment milestone breakdown included
- [ ] Timeline stated — 6 weeks from brief received
- [ ] Any flags from the call addressed (missing assets, feature gaps, price sensitivity)
- [ ] Single clear CTA — reply to go ahead

**Success measure:** The proposal could not be sent to a different prospect without rewriting it. Every section is specific to this business and this conversation.
