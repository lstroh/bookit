# Stage 1 — Step 1.3: The Discovery Call
## Wimbledon Smart Business — Complete Step-by-Step Operational Guide

**Document Version:** 1.0
**Date:** March 2026
**Status:** Active Reference
**Applies To:** All new client discovery calls

---

## What This Step Is

This is the most important 30–45 minutes in your entire sales process. Everything before it — the initial reply, the research — exists just to get you here. Done well, the call does most of the selling for you. You are not pitching. You are diagnosing.

The call has three jobs:
1. Confirm they are a good fit
2. Make them feel genuinely understood
3. End with a clear next step they have agreed to

The prospect should talk for at least 60% of the time.

---

## Before the Call — Setup

**Tools to have open before you join:**
- Bonsai — their prospect record with your pre-call research notes visible
- Jamie — start recording before you join Zoom (captures device audio, no bot joins)
- A blank note as backup (Google Docs or paper) for anything you want to flag mid-call
- The demo site loaded and logged in, ready to share screen immediately
- Zoom with your camera on

**One minute before joining:**
- Glance at your Bonsai notes — the one specific thing you planned to reference
- Remind yourself: listen first, demo second, close with a specific next step

---

## The Call Structure

The call has five phases. Each has a specific purpose. Do not rush Phase 2 to get to the demo — the demo only works when it is framed around what you just heard.

---

## Phase 1 — Open and Settle (Minutes 0–3)

Don't dive straight into questions. Take 60 seconds to make them comfortable and set the agenda. Most business owners are slightly nervous — they don't know if they're about to be pressured into something.

> *"Thanks for making time — really appreciate it. So the plan for today: I'd love to hear a bit about your business and how things currently work for you, then I'll show you what the system looks like in action. Probably 30 minutes all in, maybe a bit longer if we get into it. Does that work?"*

Setting the agenda does two things: it signals there is no pressure, and it primes them to talk first.

---

## Phase 2 — Discovery Questions (Minutes 3–22)

This is the core of the call. Ask open questions, listen carefully, and let their answers guide which question you ask next. The sections below are a bank to draw from — not a script to work through mechanically.

Reference your pre-call research naturally where relevant. Not as a show of preparation, but as a starting point that saves them having to explain the basics.

---

### Opening Question — Always Start Here

> *"Tell me a bit about the business — how long have you been running, and how does booking currently work for you?"*

This single question opens everything. Let them talk. The answer tells you their current pain, their tech comfort, and how much they've already thought about changing things.

---

### Section A — Their Business

These establish context and help you personalise the demo and proposal.

- Briefly describe what your business does — what services do you offer?
- Who are your typical customers? (age range, type of person, what problem brings them to you)
- What makes your business different from others doing similar things?
- How long have you been running, and roughly how many clients do you see per week?
- Do you have staff, or is it just you at the moment?

---

### Section B — Current Booking Setup

This is where you find the pain. Let them vent if they want to — it's useful information and builds rapport.

- How do customers currently book with you? (Phone, WhatsApp, walk-in, existing software, email)
- What happens when someone tries to book outside business hours?
- How do you manage your staff's individual schedules — is there a system, or is it informal?
- Have you had problems with double bookings or last-minute cancellations?
- Do you take deposits or payments upfront for any services?
- Have you tried any booking software before? What happened?

> **The one question that opens everything:**
> *"If you could fix one thing about how bookings work right now, what would it be?"*
>
> This is your most valuable question. The answer tells you exactly what to lead with in the demo and what to emphasise in the proposal.

---

### Section C — Website and Online Presence

This surfaces content readiness and scoping implications before you commit to a proposal.

- Do you have an existing website? Are you happy with it, or does it need replacing?
- If yes — what do you like about it? What would you change?
- Are there any websites — competitors or otherwise — whose design you admire? (Ask for URLs if possible)
- Are there any styles, colours, or elements you definitely want to avoid?
- What three words would describe the look and feel you want? (e.g. clean and modern, warm and approachable, professional and confident)
- Do you have a logo and brand colours already, or is that something you'd need help with?

---

### Section D — Content and Pages

This is where many web developers get caught out. Surface content readiness early — it directly affects your timeline and what you commit to in the proposal.

- What pages do you think you'll need? (Home, About, Services, Team, Contact — anything beyond the standard set?)
- Do you have photos of the business — premises, staff, work in action — or would we be working with stock images?
- If you have photos, are they professional shots or smartphone photos? (Both are fine — just useful to know)
- Who will provide the written content — are you happy to write a rough draft and I'll edit it, or do you need me to write it from scratch?
- Do you have a tagline or any specific wording you always use?
- Is there anything on your current site or social media you'd want to carry across?

> **Why this matters:** A client with no logo, no photos, and expecting you to write all the copy is a significantly heavier build than one who has brand assets ready. Neither is a problem — but you need to know before writing the proposal.

---

### Section E — Booking System Specifics

These questions are unique to Wimbledon Smart and essential for accurate scoping. Don't skip them.

- How many staff members need their own calendar in the system?
- Roughly how many different services do you offer?
- How long do your appointments typically run — and is there much variation?
- Do you want to take deposits? If so, a fixed amount or a percentage?
- What payment options do you want to offer? (Stripe card payments, PayPal, pay on arrival, or a mix)
- Do customers ever book the same slot regularly — weekly recurring appointments?
- Do you run any group sessions or classes with multiple people booking the same slot?
- Do you need clients to fill in any information before their appointment? (e.g. health forms, intake notes, preferences)

---

### Section F — Goals and Success

These close the discovery section and give you the language to use in the proposal.

- What does success look like for you three months after the site goes live?
- Is there a particular time of year you'd want to be live by? (seasonal rush, a planned opening, a quiet period that's a good time to launch)
- Who else is involved in this decision — is it just you, or is there a partner or manager who needs to be on board?

---

## Phase 3 — The Demo (Minutes 22–32)

Only show the demo after you have listened. You now know what matters to them — which means you can show the parts of the system that directly address what they just told you, rather than a generic walkthrough.

**How to frame it:**

> *"Based on what you've described — [reference their specific pain point] — let me show you exactly how that works."*

**What to show, in this order:**

**1. The customer booking experience (2–3 minutes)**
Open the demo site and walk through a booking as if you are their customer. Keep it brief. The point is to show how simple and professional it looks for their clients.

> *"This is what your customers see. It lives on your website, under your brand — there's no redirect to an external platform, no Fresha logo, nothing that takes them away from your business."*

**2. The business dashboard (3–4 minutes)**
Log in as the business owner. Show the calendar view, how a booking appears, how to block time, how to add a service.

> *"This is what you and your staff log into every day. Not WordPress, not a complicated admin panel — just this. Most clients are comfortable with it within the first hour."*

**3. One thing specific to their situation (1–2 minutes)**
Pick one feature that directly addresses the pain they named. Don't show everything.

- Mentioned no-shows? Show the automated reminder settings.
- Mentioned staff scheduling confusion? Show how individual calendars work.
- Mentioned taking deposits? Show the deposit configuration.
- Mentioned cancellations being a headache? Show the magic link cancellation flow.

**What not to do in the demo:**
- Don't show every feature — it overwhelms and loses the thread
- Don't apologise for anything not built yet — handle those questions cleanly (see below)
- Don't get drawn into a long technical discussion — keep moving

**If they ask about a feature that doesn't exist yet:**

> *"That's on the roadmap — not in the current version, but it's planned for later this year. I can make a note and let you know when it's ready. Would that work as a workaround in the meantime: [offer a practical alternative]?"*

---

## Phase 4 — Soft Qualification (Minutes 32–38)

Before you close, confirm the practical details you need to write an accurate proposal.

**Practical scoping:**
- How many staff members need their own calendar? (confirm if not already covered)
- Roughly how many services will be listed?
- Do you have a domain name already, or will you need one?
- Do you have a logo file and brand colours ready to go, or is that something we'd need to sort first?

**Fit and readiness:**
- Does what you have seen feel like roughly what you had in mind?
- Is there anything you would need that you have not seen today?
- What is your rough timeline — are you looking to get started in the next few weeks, or is this more exploratory at this stage?

That last question is important. It surfaces whether they are a genuine near-term prospect or someone still browsing. Both are fine — but you need to know so you can calibrate your follow-up.

---

## Phase 5 — Close With Next Steps (Minutes 38–42)

Never end the call without agreeing on a specific next step. Vague endings lose deals. Specific endings with a date keep momentum.

**Standard close:**

> *"This sounds like a really good fit. I'll put together a proposal for you — it'll be a personal email, not a generic PDF, covering exactly what I'd build for your business and the pricing. I'll have it with you by [tomorrow / the day after tomorrow]. If you have any questions when you read it, just reply and we can jump on a quick call to go through it. Does that work?"*

**If they seem hesitant or non-committal:**

> *"It sounds like you might have a few more questions before you're ready for a proposal — that's completely fine. What would be most useful right now?"*

**If they want to go straight to pricing on the call:**

Don't dodge it. Give them the numbers clearly.

> *"Happy to go through pricing now. There's a one-off setup fee of £995, then £99 a month for hosting, maintenance, and support — that covers everything, no hidden extras. If you pay annually upfront, the setup drops to £495, which saves you £500 in Year 1. Month-to-month after that, cancel anytime with 30 days notice. The proposal will lay all of this out clearly with a full breakdown."*

**If they ask about the introductory rate (first two clients):**

> *"For the first couple of clients I'm offering a reduced setup fee of £495 in exchange for a testimonial and a short case study once you've been live for a few weeks. The monthly stays at £99. That's the deal if you want it."*

## After the Call — Immediately

Do these three things before anything else while the call is still fresh. Jamie will have generated a summary — use it as the source for your Bonsai notes rather than relying on memory.

### 1. Update Bonsai (5 minutes)

Add a call summary to the prospect record using this template:

```
CALL DATE: [date]
CURRENT BOOKING SETUP: [what they're using now]
KEY PAIN POINT: [the one thing they most want to fix]
BUSINESS TYPE: [industry, team size]
SERVICES: [rough count and duration range]
STAFF NEEDING ACCESS: [number]
PAYMENT MODEL: [deposit / upfront / on arrival / mix]
CONTENT READINESS: [logo yes/no, photos yes/no, copy — they write / I write / mixed]
PAGES BEYOND STANDARD: [any additional pages requested]
TIMELINE: [when they want to go live]
DECISION MAKER: [just them / others involved]
FLAGS: [anything unusual — scope risk, low tech comfort, missing assets, feature gaps]
NEXT STEP: [proposal by X date]
```

Update status to **Proposal Sent** once the proposal goes out (Step 1.5).

### 2. Create the Google Drive folder structure (2 minutes)

If not already created, set this up now:

```
[Business Name]/
  ├── Brief & Assets/
  ├── Proposal/
  ├── Contracts/
  └── Build/
```

### 3. Set the proposal task in Bonsai (1 minute)

Create a task: *"Send proposal — [Business Name]"* due tomorrow morning. Do not leave it open-ended.

## Handling Common Objections On the Call

These will come up. Have the responses ready before you join.

### "That's more expensive than Bookly (£89)"

> *"You're right — Bookly is £89. But Bookly is just a plugin. You still need to build the website yourself, configure everything, and train your staff on WordPress admin. We build everything for you. Your staff log into a clean dashboard that takes five minutes to learn. You're comparing a plugin to a complete solution — they're not the same thing. How much is 20 hours of your time worth?"*

### "Fresha is free — why would I pay?"

> *"Fresha is free until you read the small print. They take 20% of every new client booking. If you're bringing in £200 a week in new clients, that's £40 a week — over £2,000 a year — going to Fresha. With us, you keep 100% of your revenue. After 18 months, we're actually cheaper than free. And your customers don't have to create a Fresha account just to book with you."*

### "Can you lower the setup fee?"

> *"The setup fee covers the full build — reducing it would mean reducing scope. What I can offer is the annual plan, which drops the setup fee to £495 and saves you £500 overall in Year 1."*

### "I need to think about it"

> *"Of course — it's an important decision. Can I ask what specifically you're weighing up? If there's a particular feature or concern, I'd rather address it now than leave you with an open question."*

### "I already have a website — I just need the booking system"

> *"Totally understood. The booking system does sit within a complete website package in how we currently offer it — partly because the dashboard and the site need to work together. That said, let me understand your current site a bit better and we can talk about what makes sense. What platform is it on, and how happy are you with it?"*

### "Do you have any examples of sites you've built?"

If you have the demo site live:
> *"Yes — let me show you. This is a demo I built to show the full experience. [Share screen.] Obviously your version would be in your branding, but this gives you the idea of the quality and how the booking flow works."*

If you don't have the demo live yet:
> *"I'm finishing up the demo site right now — it'll be live this week. Happy to send you the link when it's up so you can click through it yourself. In the meantime, I can walk you through the dashboard on screen if you'd like to see it now."*
